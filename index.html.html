HS vs HTS vs Schedule B: A Practical Guide to U.S. Trade Classification

HS, HTS, and Schedule B codes are closely related, but they are not interchangeable. Understanding where each system applies can help businesses avoid classification mistakes when importing or exporting products.

The Short Version

HS Code International six-digit foundation for product classification.
HTS Code U.S. classification system used for imports.
Schedule B U.S. classification system used for exports.
Key point: The systems share the international HS foundation, but classifications can differ beyond the first six digits.

What Is an HS Code?

The Harmonized System (HS) is the international framework used to classify traded goods. The internationally harmonized portion reaches six digits.

  • 2 digits: Chapter
  • 4 digits: Heading
  • 6 digits: HS subheading

Countries can extend the classification beyond six digits to meet national tariff and statistical requirements. That is why a complete tariff number from one country should not automatically be copied into another country's customs documentation.

What Is an HTS Code?

The Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTS) is the classification system used for goods imported into the United States. It is based on the international HS structure and adds U.S.-specific classification detail.

An overseas supplier may provide an HS-based tariff number that is useful as a starting point, but that does not automatically make the complete number the correct U.S. HTS classification.

What HTS Classification Supports

  • Classification of imported merchandise
  • Applicable tariff treatment
  • Customs entry documentation
  • Import statistical reporting
  • Identification of relevant provisions in the U.S. tariff schedule

What Is a Schedule B Code?

Schedule B is the U.S. export classification system used for export reporting. Like HTS, it is built on the international HS framework.

The important distinction is the direction of trade:

U.S. import → HTS
U.S. export → Schedule B
International reference → HS

Why Supplier-Provided Codes Can Cause Problems

A supplier outside the United States may classify a product according to its own country's tariff schedule. The first six HS digits may align with the U.S. classification, while the additional national digits may not.

Therefore, an importer should not assume that a supplier's complete tariff number is automatically the correct U.S. import classification.

Why Classification Becomes a Data Problem

Classification is relatively manageable for a small product catalog. It becomes much harder when a business manages hundreds or thousands of products.

A scalable process may need to maintain:

  • Product descriptions
  • Technical specifications
  • Material composition
  • Intended use
  • Supporting documentation
  • Classification decisions
  • Review and approval records
  • Historical classification data

At that scale, classification is not simply a tariff-code lookup. It becomes a product data, workflow, and compliance management problem.

Common Classification Mistakes

1. Treating HS and HTS as synonyms

HS is the international six-digit foundation, while HTS refers to the U.S. import classification system.

2. Copying a supplier's complete tariff code

A supplier may be using another country's national tariff extensions. Verify the classification required for the U.S. import.

3. Treating HTS and Schedule B as interchangeable

They share the HS foundation but serve different U.S. trade purposes.

4. Treating classification as a one-time lookup

Product information, tariff schedules, and classification requirements can change. Businesses with large catalogs need a process for review and maintaining classification history.

Practical Takeaway

Remember: HS is the international foundation. HTS is used for U.S. imports. Schedule B is used for U.S. exports.

The first six digits connect the systems, but that does not mean the complete classifications are interchangeable. Businesses should determine the classification system relevant to the country and direction of trade before using a code in customs or export documentation.
